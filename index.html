Abstract

    This article discusses how the terrestrial planets in our solar system formed

Introduction


According to the N-body simulations of Kokubo & Ida (1995, 1996, 1998) using the GRAvity PipE (GRAPE) 
specialised hardware and the current dynamical model’s terrestrial planet formation, the terrestrial 
planets in our solar system, formed from planetesimal or pebble accretion (Brasser, 2025). These 
planetesimals form from the protoplanetary disk and when they combine with enough other planetesimals
they form protoplanets which become our terrestrial planets.

Analysis and discussion

The beginning of all our terrestrial planets
      
All the terrestrial planets in our solar system began the same way, as a large horizontal disk of 
gas around the sun known as the sun’s gaseous protoplanetary disk. This disk was present
when the giant planets migrated into a compact orbital configuration in a chain of resonances
(Liu Beibei, Raymond Sean N., Jacobson Seth A)  (the frequency of their orbits have a common ratio).
The protoplanetary disk was a large circular disk around the sun made of mainly hydrogen and
helium gases mixed with tiny amounts of tiny bits of dust metals and rocks, where the terrestrial 
planets form from. As the dust rocks and metals collide in the disk they begin to combine and grow 
(accretion) when they become big enough (1km +) they become planetesimals. Planetesimals are 
celestial objects made of dust and rock and are formed by accretion and held together by their 
gravity. Accretional collisions between 1 to 1000km planetesimals lead to the gradual 
build up of lunar to mars size protoplanets, and then the terrestrial planets are believed 
to have formed by violent collisions of tens of lunar- to Mars-size protoplanets at time
t < 200 Myr after the protoplanetary gas disk dispersal (t0) (Nesvorný David,

The Earth and Moon
 
Scientists believe that Earth, like the other inner planets, came to its current state
in three different stages. The first stage is known as accretion, or the formation of
a planet from the existing particles within the solar system as they collided with each 
other to form larger and larger bodies. Scientists believe the next stage involved the 
collision of a protoplanet (Theia) with a very young planet Earth. This is thought to have 
occurred more than 4.5 billion years ago.  Then millions of years later as the earth cools, 
liquid water forms on the surface and the earth settled into its current layers, inner 
core, outer core, mantle and the crust. Many believe in the mega impact theory. 
The mega impact theory was that the moon was formed by the collision between earth and
a body around the size of mars called Theia, and the silicate mantle of earth that was 
ejected along with remnants of the Theia formed the moon. This theory does explain some 
aspects of the Moon’s properties quite well, such as its mass and orbit, but with some major 
caveats. One outstanding mystery has been why the composition of the Moon is so similar 
to Earth’s. Scientists can study the composition of a material based on its isotopic 
signature, a chemical clue to how and where an object was created. The lunar samples 
scientists have been able to study in labs show very similar isotopic signatures to rocks 
from Earth, unlike rocks from Mars or elsewhere in the solar system. This makes it likely 
that much of the material that makes up the Moon originally came from Earth. In this
theory where Theia sprayed out into orbit and mixed with only a little material from Earth,
it is less likely we would see such strong similarities – unless Theia was also isotopically 
similar to Earth, an unlikely coincidence (Frank Tavares).

Venus
 
The formation of Venus is very similar to the way the earth formed, when in the protoplanetary
disk dust rocks and metals collided and grew through the process of accretion and now it 
has a very similar size and structure to earth. Each planet has an iron core enveloped by
a hot-rock mantle; the thinnest of skins forms a rocky, exterior crust. On both planets, 
this thin skin changes form and sometimes erupts into volcanoes in response to the ebb and 
flow of heat and pressure deep beneath (NASA).

Mars
 
Recent high-precision meteoritic data infers that Mars finished its accretion rapidly within
10 Myr of the beginning of the Solar System and had an accretion zone that did not entirely
overlap with the Earth’s. Mars accreted very rapidly; approximately 50% of its mass after
2 Myr and finished most of its accretion between 5 and 10 Myr after the formation of the 
solar system (Woo Jason Man Yin, Stadel Joachim, Grimm Simon, Brasser Ramon).

Mercury

Mercury formed about 4.5 billion years ago when gravity pulled swirling gas and dust together 
to form this small planet nearest the Sun (the process called accretion). Like its fellow 
terrestrial planets, Mercury has a central core, a rocky mantle, and a solid crust. Mercury 
is the second densest planet, after Earth. It has a large metallic core with a radius of about
1,289 miles (2,074 kilometres), about 85% of the planet's radius. There is evidence that it is
partly molten or liquid. Mercury's outer shell, comparable to Earth's outer shell 
(called the mantle and crust), is only about 400 kilometres (250 miles) thick.
